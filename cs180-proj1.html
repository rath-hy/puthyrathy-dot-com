Overview

Sergei Prokudin-Gorskii photographed the Russian Empire in the early 1900s using a camera that captured three exposures of the same scene through blue, green, and red filters, stacked into one glass-plate negative. To get a color photo back, you split the negative into its three channels and stack them — except the plates don't line up perfectly, since the exposures happened seconds apart on a hand-loaded camera. This project finds the right pixel offset to align the green and red channels to the blue one, first by brute-force search on small JPEGs, then with a coarse-to-fine image pyramid for the full-resolution scans.

Part 1: Single-scale alignment

cathedral.jpg, monastery.jpg, tobolsk.jpg

Before building the pyramid, we validate the exhaustive-search strategy on its own: for every integer displacement (dy, dx) in [-15, 15], shift the channel and score it against the blue channel with L2 distance, keep the best-scoring shift. These three JPEGs are small enough (~340-400px tall per channel) that this is fast and doesn't need a pyramid.

Part 2: Multi-scale pyramid alignment

The provided TIFFs are up to ~9600×3600px per plate — searching a window wide enough to cover their true offsets at full resolution would be far too slow. pyramid_align downsamples both channels by half repeatedly until they're under 200px on the short side, does one exhaustive search there, then climbs back up doubling the offset and refining it with a small local search at each level. Final method used below: gradient magnitude (edge strength) instead of raw pixels, scored with L2 — offsets are (dy, dx), green and red relative to blue.

Why church and emir break the obvious approaches

L2 does well for all files except church.tif, whereas NCC performs well for everything besides emir.tif. church.tif is dominated by large flat regions of similar brightness (sky/water). L2 is pulled towards whatever shift minimizes this disagreement, which isn't necessarily what lines up the church and shoreline best. NCC subtracts the mean and normalizes, so it's invariant to the brightness gap between channels. As for emir.tif, the emir's robe is a busy, fine pattern that aliases into high-frequency noise when downsampled. NCC is a pattern-correlation measure, so it's vulnerable to false correlation peaks there. L2 survives because the robe is much darker/higher-contrast in R than in B, so raw-intensity matching has a well-defined true minimum.

To make one method work everywhere, we align on edges instead of raw pixels — a gradient-magnitude map is bright wherever the image is changing fast and dark where it's flat, which sidesteps both problems: it throws out the raw brightness that confuses L2, and it isn't fooled by the alised texture that confuses NCC.

We also compared gradients+L2 against gradients+NCC across all 14 provided images: every single image landed on the exact same offset under both losses, so there's no quality tradeoff between them once you're aligning on gradients. L2 was ~1.8-2x faster, so that's the final method used throughout this page.
